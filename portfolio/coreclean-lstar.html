Downcore colour at the Shackleton Site

Sediment lightness down 166 m of core from IODP Site U1385 on the Iberian Margin (about 1.4 million years). It was measured from core photographs before and after CoreClean masked out cracks, burrows and drilling disturbance, and is shown above the shipboard spectrophotometer record. What’s shown

  • Unfiltered image record: mean colour across the width of each core photograph at every depth, with the within-row standard deviation, from the stitched, background-removed composite.
  • Filtered image record: the same, using only pixels the SegNet model classed as undisturbed sediment. Depths where more than half the row was masked are left blank. The hover readout gives the fraction of pixels kept.
  • Spectrophotometer record: the shipboard L* splice at 1 cm from Hodell et al. (2015), with its LR04 age model shown in the readout.

This is the corrected record. The version in the original report was built from section images that had been stitched rotated, so each section’s record ran upside down, and sections were joined in string order (1, 10, 11, …, 2, 20, …). Both errors only reordered rows, so the record has been rebuilt exactly from the original measurements.

All three records are on the revised composite depth scale (rmcd) of the Hodell et al. (2015) splice. Each photographed section starts where the splice table puts it. Its exact position is then fitted by correlating its filtered L* against the spectrophotometer record, keeping sections in core order within their splice interval with no overlaps. Most sections move by only a few centimetres (median 5 cm). The largest moves, up to 0.65 m, are in intervals where the photographs are shorter than the curated core. Blank stretches are places with no usable photograph.

Section U1385A-10H-2 (87.3–88.8 m) is left out of the image records. The sediment there is a little darker (about 4 L* in the spectrophotometer record), but the photograph is about 13 L* darker again, relative to the spectrophotometer, than any other section. That points to an exposure problem with the photograph, not real sediment colour.

Image colour values come from an uncalibrated camera, so compare their shapes with the spectrophotometer record, not their absolute values.

COMA

Darkfield

As is perhaps clear from the rest of the contents of this site, I am a nerd who is massively interested in immersive and interactive theatre. So, even before getting to the Fringe, I thought I would find the cool, experimental immersive experiences and book them into my schedule. Unfortunately COMA and FLIGHT felt more like listening to an ASMR youtube video than a theatre production, and both left me wanting something more to justify the exciting and polished production.

★★

Climbing Into the Box

I experienced COMA first; walking down the off-white corridor between the triple decker bunks, the tension in the room was palpable. The slightly disconcerting hospital decor, combined with the low sounds and the novelty of stepping into a shipping container after a brief warning from a crew about the effect of complete darkness meant that the crowd was quiet and nervous. Climbing into a top bunk, I put on a pair of headphones. Minor issue, but they were wired such that the left and right were on the wrong side unless you allowed the cable to drag over or under your body. The claustrophobic space, with hospital greebles like a vent and a small reading light, was coldly functional, with the eerie off-white decor preparing me for what I hoped would be an unsettling experience.

The Pill

During the first 10 minutes, a brief discussion from the Doctor, voiced by Simon Kane, suggests to the participant to take a pill located on a small metal dish located by their head. I will admit to having missed the dish while the lights were up, and scrabbling for it in the total darkness that Darkfield bill all of their experiences on was a moment of genuine worriedness. With some instructions just vague enough to allow people to not take the pill if they wanted, we were told that this small capsule will do nothing, but maybe prepare us for a collective experience. I like the idea of using a physical act as a liminal point; preparing the audience for immersion, but the long drawn out narration about the pill made it feel more like I was being needled into taking my medicine than decisively leaving my body behind.

Strong Perfume

Kane's voice appears again, this time closer, with a very convincing series of audio effects implying that he was walking down the hall, checking other participants for whether they had taken the pill. As the audio projects him by the bed, the sound of a drawn curtain, and the injected scent of perfume try to convince you he is there. Unfortunately, this convincing did not work on me. I could not help thinking that I am just lying inside a dark shipping container, waiting for something to happen. Perhaps being too aware of my body, or not having a moment at which the production tells me where I am to imagine myself, meant that I was still just Sam, lying in a top bunk.

Buzzing Flies

A fifteen minute repetition of the same tricks, walking back and forth, talking about pills, and buzzing flies and strong scents follows. Obviously by the name, we are meant to be in some sort of coma, and there is a distinctly medical feel to the whole affair, but it fails to say anything that you think might be raised by the immobility, helplessness, and blindness that you are actually placed in during the experience. A short story told about a woman having pills forced down her throat made me think that I would experience something like that sense of violation or helplessness. Obviously this is not something you can do in a show like this, but it prompts the question of what the story is hoping to do.

Bass Drop

Immersive theatre is about being placed into a story or experience; feeling the emotions that someone in that scenario would feel. COMA does not give you a character, or a setup, or even a location, besides a hospital bunk, and the implication behind its name. You hear about medical patients, but their stories are simply recounted.

In a final act of surprise, large subwoofers shake the beds with vibration effects. I wonder whether this was trying to make us feel helpless beside some sort of sublime power of audio tricks. As you can maybe tell from my tone, I found it unimpressive, and the closing line reminding me not to leave my body behind was not needed.

Out of the Box

The production was exquisite; audio was crisp and directional, the smells were realistic, the set was a convincing, not quite real, reproduction of cramped hospital beds; but it was let down by the meandering story being told to you. It felt less like experiencing immersion and more like listening to an audiobook in bed.

While I understand darkness is the gimmick that allows Darkfield to try and create the imagined worlds they want to create, it seemed a shame that as soon as the lights went out, I was not able to see the set, and instead was just thinking, and overthinking, what was going on. Should I take this pill? Who am I? Am I meant to respond when the doctor talks to me? I did not think so!

Accessibility and Safety

There is a brief warm up period, where the darkness is tested, after which participants are given the chance to leave. There was not any provision for personal emergencies after that point, which I felt was an omission compared to the tools at similar experiences.

The bunks were stacked three high, though the ones at the base were accessible without any steps, and the corridor was wide enough for larger powered wheelchairs. The audio nature of the performance would make it hard to experience for those with auditory impairments. It requires no reading or close visual acuity.

Underwhelming

In the end, I just lay in a box for 30 minutes. It was a pretty box, and I liked the way it could inject smells and sounds to create an atmosphere, but I am not sure they were trying to say anything more than come lie in our dark box for a while, and that is disappointing for such a promising production.

- Sam Hutton
